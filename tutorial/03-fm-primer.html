3. FM synthesis in 10 minutes

Operators, algorithms, feedback, envelopes

The big idea

A normal synthesizer starts with a rich waveform (like a sawtooth) and carves it with a filter. An FM synthesizer starts with the simplest waveform there is, a sine wave (a pure tone), and makes it richer by wobbling its pitch extremely fast using another sine wave. That is frequency modulation, the “FM”.

When the wobble is slow you hear vibrato. When the wobble is in the audio range (hundreds of times a second), the vibrato disappears and new overtones appear: the tone turns bright, metallic or bell-like. Louder wobble = more overtones. Which overtones you get depends on the speed of the wobble relative to the note.

Operators: carriers and modulators

The building block is the operator: one sine oscillator with its own volume envelope. The FM-1 has six, numbered 1–6. Each operator plays one of two roles:

  • A carrier is heard. Its output goes to the speaker.
  • A modulator is not heard directly. Its output wobbles the pitch of another operator.

A modulator’s level acts as a brightness control for whatever it modulates. A carrier’s level is a volume control.

Operator, voice, preset

Roles are decided by the algorithm, not by the operator. The same operator can be a carrier in one algorithm and a modulator in another. A complete sound (algorithm + all settings of the six operators) is called a voice in DX7 terms and a preset on the FM-1’s panel. In this guide, “voice” and “preset” are the same thing.

Frequency ratios

Each operator’s pitch is set as a ratio to the note you play. Ratio 1 = the note itself; 2 = an octave up; 0.5 = an octave down; 3 = an octave plus a fifth.

  • When a modulator has a whole-number ratio (1, 2, 3…) compared with its carrier, the result sounds harmonic: like an organ, a flute or an electric piano.
  • When the ratio is not a whole number (such as 3.5 or 1.41), the overtones fall between the harmonics and the sound is inharmonic: bells, gongs, metallic percussion.

On the FM-1 the ratio is built from FreqCoarse (the whole part) and FreqFine (the decimals), plus a tiny Detune. Coarse 0 means ratio 0.5. An operator can also be set to Fixed frequency, where it ignores the key you play and stays at one pitch in Hz; that is used for percussion “thumps” and noise-like effects.

How the screen shows the frequency

Coarse and Fine are shown as separate numbers (0–31 and 0–99). Neither the manual nor the photos show a combined “ratio” readout, so you have to do the arithmetic yourself, using standard DX7 rules: Coarse 1 + Fine 0 = ratio 1.00; each Fine step adds about 1% of the Coarse value.

Level = brightness

This is the most important rule in FM: the louder a modulator, the brighter (and noisier) the sound. Take any sound, turn up the modulator’s OutLevel and it gets more metallic; turn it down and it becomes a pure sine again.

Algorithms: who modulates whom

An algorithm is a wiring diagram for the six operators. The FM-1 has the same 32 as the DX7. Read the diagram top to bottom:

  • A line from operator A down to operator B means A modulates B.
  • Operators on the bottom row, joined to OUT, are carriers.
  • A red arrow looping back on an operator is its feedback.

Rules of thumb:

  • Long chains (algorithms 1–2, 16–18): complex, evolving, aggressive. Good for bass, brass, electric pianos.
  • Several short stacks (algorithms 5–15): layered sounds, with different timbres in the same voice.
  • Many carriers, few modulators (algorithms 21–32): organ and string-like, close to additive synthesis.

All 32 diagrams are on the reference page. To change algorithm: turn ALGORITHM.

Feedback

In every algorithm one operator is wired to listen to itself (or, in a few cases, to a neighbour), so its output modulates its own pitch. This is Feedback, a single value from 0 to 7 on the voice (EDIT → GLO).

  • 0: the operator is a pure sine.
  • 1–3: it gets brighter, towards a sawtooth.
  • 5–7: very bright, rasping and finally noisy.

Feedback is a cheap way to get “saw”-like and noisy timbres from one operator, which is why it matters for leads and basses.

Envelopes

Every operator has an envelope generator (EG): a four-stage shape that controls how its level changes while a key is held and after it is released. The DX7-style EG has four rates (how fast each stage moves) and four levels (where each stage ends).

  1. Key down: the level moves from L4 (the starting level) to L1 at speed R1.
  2. Then from L1 to L2 at R2.
  3. Then from L2 to L3 at R3. The envelope stays at L3 while you hold the key (this is the sustain level).
  4. Key up: the level moves from L3 to L4 at R4 (the release).

Higher rate numbers = faster. Levels run 0–99.

Two uses of that envelope:

  • On a carrier it is a volume contour: a piano is quick-attack with a long decay; a pad is slow-attack with a slow release.
  • On a modulator it is a brightness contour: a bright “pluck” at the start that mellows out (a classic electric piano or bell), or a slow brightness swell.

The pitch of the whole voice has its own four-stage envelope (the Pitch EG), used for pitch-drop effects. And on top of all that the FM-1 adds one simple global Attack-Decay-Sustain-Release envelope you can switch on with the ENV button (see page 6).

Other ingredients

  • Keyboard level scaling makes an operator quieter or louder towards the top or bottom of the keyboard, around a break point note. Use it so high notes do not get harsh.
  • Rate scaling makes envelopes faster on high notes, like a real instrument (a high piano string dies away sooner than a low one).
  • Velocity sensitivity makes an operator louder when the note is hit harder.
  • LFO (low frequency oscillator): a slow wobble applied to pitch (vibrato) and/or amplitude (tremolo).
  • Detune slightly offsets an operator’s pitch; with two similar operators it creates chorus-like beating.
